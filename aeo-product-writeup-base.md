## Part 2 — The fuller write-up (draft one-pager)

### The shift

Search is splitting in two. People still type queries into Google, but a growing share never see a link — they get an answer. In the first four months of 2026, **68% of US Google searches ended without a click**, up from 60% in 2024. AI Overviews reach **2.5 billion monthly users**, and Google's AI Mode has passed **1 billion**; the two are now merged into a single Search experience. AI Overviews appear on **20%+ of all Google searches**, and on **48% of commercially-oriented query sets**.

Where an AI Overview appears, organic click-through runs **8% versus 15% where it doesn't** — close to half.

**One honest caveat, and it's worth being the person who raises it.** The decline isn't a straight line down. Seer Interactive, tracking 53 brands across 5.5M queries, found AI-Overview click-through *recovering* from 1.31% in December 2025 to 2.36% by February 2026. Google maintains overall click volume is stable. Nobody has published what happened after February. So the accurate statement isn't "clicks are collapsing" — it's that the relationship between rank, visibility and traffic has come apart, and last-click organic sessions no longer tell you what's happening to your discovery.

**It isn't one engine any more, either.** ChatGPT's share of gen-AI web traffic fell from ~76% in mid-2025 to **~53% by May 2026**. Gemini went from ~9% to ~27% and crossed **1 billion monthly active users** in August 2026. Claude went from ~2% to ~9%. Anyone who built an "optimise for ChatGPT" plan last year is now addressing about half the audience.

And the volume is genuinely front-loaded toward younger buyers: **60% of Gen Z regularly use AI Overviews versus 29% of boomers**; 28% of Gen Z use generative AI for shopping versus 16% (McKinsey, five-market survey, n=4,863, June 2026). Whether this is urgent for a given company depends a lot on who buys from them.

### The thing most teams get wrong

Here's the finding that reframes the work. Across LLM citations measured from October 2025 to May 2026, **only about 1% of the sources cited by LLMs are brand-owned websites**. Even inside the ten most-cited domains, brand sites account for 3–10%.

Which means a strategy that consists of publishing more pages on your own domain is aimed at 1% of the surface. Getting cited requires presence where the engines actually look — third-party sites, communities, review platforms, structured data the models can parse — and most teams have no programme for any of it.

Related: Adobe's own AI-readability checker scored the average US retail product page at **66%**, meaning a third of the content on a typical page isn't cleanly parseable by machines. Most sites have basic, fixable eligibility gaps and don't know it.

### The gap everyone has and nobody has filled

Three independent 2026 surveys land on the same finding:

- **82%** of teams have started doing AEO in some form — only **7.7%** have a dedicated team or program for it.
- **85%** say AI has changed their search strategy — only **22%** have integrated it into how they actually work, and only **9%** can measure the metrics that matter. **40% still track it by manually typing prompts into ChatGPT.**
- **94%** of enterprise CMOs are increasing investment this year, averaging **12% of digital budget**.

Universal strategic buy-in. Almost no execution capacity. Budget arriving with nowhere to land.

It's showing up as churn, too — SEO tools were the **most-replaced martech category** in 2025, with 37% of buyers citing AI capability as a reason to switch.

### Why the existing options don't close it

- **Doing it yourself with AI tools** works up to a point. Then you hit a ceiling — quality, time, or the fact that the surface keeps moving and nobody has bandwidth to track it. This is the most common state among companies we talk to.
- **Agencies** can do the work but are learning AI search on the customer's dime. Mid-market retainers run **$2,500–$15,000+/month**, with a 3–6 month ramp and limited visibility into what's actually being done. Most aren't charging separately for AI-search work at all — 43% fold it into existing packages at no premium, which tells you how seriously it's being resourced.
- **Monitoring tools** — Peec, Otterly, Ahrefs Brand Radar, and a long tail of around thirty trackers — tell you where you're visible across engines. Useful, and a dead end on their own: the dashboard doesn't change the answer.
- **The platforms that do execute are enterprise-shaped.** Profound is a $1B-valuation company selling demo-led into Fortune 500 marketing teams. Adobe closed its $1.9B Semrush acquisition in April 2026 and bundles AI-visibility into its enterprise suite. Sitecore bought Scrunch for ~$225M. Real capability — priced and staffed for companies far above $1M–$12M ARR, and all of it assumes the customer already has people to act on what the platform surfaces.

The unserved middle is a literate team that knows what needs doing, can't staff it, and isn't an enterprise account.

### What Berlin is

Berlin is an AI-search engine that a human operator drives in partnership with an agent. The operator brings judgment and intent; Berlin brings the brand's full context, the data, the reusable procedures, and the execution.

It's deliberately human-in-the-loop. An operator working with an agent — not an agent running unattended.

**The loop is Audit → Report → Act.**

- **Audit.** Berlin watches a brand-shaped slice of the search and AI-answer surface, composed from a vetted library of strategy patterns maintained by our research team. The composition is what makes it personalised — a brand-new site mostly needs content work; an established brand with weak topical authority needs review presence, citations, and authority tracking; a regulated industry needs a different shape again.
- **Report.** Signal is synthesised into a living per-brand report — findings and prioritised focus areas, kept current as new signal lands. This is what a stakeholder actually looks at, and how they track KPIs over time.
- **Act.** From the report, Berlin generates discrete, well-scoped actions, each tied to a specific finding — content to create, content to update, technical fixes, off-site citation work, validation. Many execute automatically against connected systems; the rest route through a review queue for approval.

It's a cycle, not an onboarding sequence.

### What sits underneath

- **A brand profile Berlin builds itself.** On adding a domain, it researches the brand from the open internet and the brand's own site: what it sells, business model, company size, target segments, geographies, competitors, and a hierarchical topic tree of what the brand is actually about. The customer reviews and corrects it. Everything downstream is grounded in this.
- **Brand files.** Anything that doesn't fit a structured field — tone, keyword priorities, internal playbooks, style guides, product taxonomies — is attached as a file the agent pulls on demand. This is how a customer's own knowledge gets into the work.
- **Its own keyword and crawl data.** Keyword volume, difficulty and ranking data cached in Berlin's own database, so customers don't need their own Semrush or Ahrefs subscriptions. Every page of the brand's site *and its competitors' sites* crawled on a recurring cadence with full version history.
- **A semantic index over both.** Point at an idea and get the keyword cluster around it with volumes. Check instantly whether the brand already has a page covering a topic before commissioning a new one. See what changed on any competitor since the last crawl, and what they just published.
- **Live, multi-engine access — not a stale index.** Google Search, ChatGPT search and Perplexity search are exposed directly to the agent, so Berlin inspects what the engines say *right now* about the brand and its competitors. Given how fast Gemini has taken share, breadth here is the point.
- **Off-site surfaces.** Given that ~1% of LLM citations come from brand-owned sites, this is not a nice-to-have. Reddit, LinkedIn, YouTube and others tracked for the brand *and* its competitors.
- **Saved procedures.** When the operator works out how to do something well, it's saved as a reusable skill available across every account. Method compounds instead of being re-derived per customer.
- **Scheduled deterministic jobs.** The cheap, repetitive, high-volume analysis runs on a schedule with no LLM in the loop — so it costs nothing per run and can run continuously. Agents then read that output and do the judgement-heavy part. This is how continuous coverage stays affordable.

### Integrations

Connected once per brand: Google Search Console, Google Analytics, Bing Webmaster Tools, CMS platforms, and social accounts including LinkedIn and Reddit. That's what lets Berlin publish and fix, not just recommend.

### Oversight

Before an action touches a live system, Berlin surfaces what it intends to do. Teams configure how much oversight they want — every action, or only certain categories. Progress, reports and actions surface in a dashboard the customer's team watches. Multiple brands can sit in one org, with shared access and permissions.

## Part 3 — Who to look for, and how to ask

### The profile

Our current working hypothesis, deliberately broad. We're narrowing it through exactly these conversations, so an imperfect-but-close intro is still worth having.

**Strong signals:**

- **SaaS company.** Our chosen first vertical.
- **$80k–$1M monthly revenue** (~$1M–$12M ARR). The lower bound matters more than the upper.
- **Already doing real SEO** — not starting from zero — but with a small marketing team, or a founder doing it personally.
- **Already using AI** for that work, and feeling both the leverage and the ceiling of it.
- **The owner understands search well.** A literate buyer who wants to see and operate the platform, not hand it off blind.

**Triggers that make a conversation land:**

- Agency contract coming up for renewal
- Traffic or ranking drop, often AI-Overview-related
- Anxiety about AI search specifically — "we're invisible in ChatGPT"
- Realising they've optimised for ChatGPT and have no idea where they stand in Gemini
- Their buyers skew young — the generational gap in AI-search usage makes this urgent sooner
- Funding round and new growth targets
- A competitor showing up in AI answers where they don't
- Burned by a freelancer or agency, no trust left

**Not a fit right now:**

- Non-SaaS (eCommerce, marketplaces, publishers, services) — not because it can't work, but because we're focused
- Below ~$80k MRR — budget and search maturity usually aren't there
- No real SEO activity yet
- Agencies reselling SEO — different motion entirely
- "Just exploring" with no trigger

**Two buyer shapes**, wanting different things:

| | Founder-owned | Marketing-leader-owned |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Founder personally does SEO, small or no team | Head of Marketing / SEO lead with a small team and a budget line |
| Company | 2–15 people | 20–100+ |
| What they want | To get past their own ceiling, keep their hands on it | Output, visibility, accountability |
| Current spend | $200–$2,000/mo on tools and freelancers | $2.5k–$15k/mo on agency plus tools |

### The ask

Three ways to frame it. **Pick one — don't offer all three.**

**A. Design partner.** "We're taking a small number of design partners. Preferential pricing locked in, direct input on the roadmap, weekly access to the product team for the first eight weeks, priority on feature requests. What we need back: a 30-minute call weekly for eight weeks, one workflow walkthrough, honest feedback, and genuine willingness to actually run their search work through Berlin."

**B. Scoped proof of value.** "Give us one topic area and your biggest problem around it. We'll produce the strategy and one real execution artifact on your actual site within a week. If it's useful we talk; if not, you got a free audit." — Rules: book the follow-up before doing the work. One topic, one artifact, no scope creep.

**C. Soft.** Just a 30-minute conversation. Lowest friction, right when the friend's relationship with the target is light and you don't want them spending social capital on a pitch.

**Default:** B for a strong fit who's skeptical. A for a strong fit already leaning in. C for a cold-ish introduction.

## Part 4 — How a pilot gets judged

This is the part to get right, because the honest answer to "will this work" is "it depends on your sector, and we'll measure it." That's a stronger position than a promised multiplier, and it's the thing most vendors in this category can't say.

### Don't promise a conversion multiple

There is no defensible cross-market number for how much better AI-referred traffic converts. The verified range across sectors runs from AI converting **54% better** (US retail, Adobe, May 2026) to **28% worse** (travel, same dataset, same month). Anyone quoting a single universal multiplier is extrapolating from one vertical.

What to say instead:

> "AI-referred visitors arrive further down the funnel and convert better in most sectors where it's been measured properly — Adobe, on over a trillion US retail visits, puts the gap at +54%. But it varies enormously by category, and in travel it's currently negative. The honest answer for your business is that we'd measure it on your own data in the first few weeks."

Same for volume. AI referrals are still small — around **0.25% of total site traffic** on a broad web average, rising to **1.5–1.7% in verticals like SMB software, insurance and finance**. The case isn't that this is big today. It's the growth rate, the quality of the visitor, and the fact that AI Overviews influence decisions without generating any trackable referral at all.

### What we'd actually measure

A pilot should be judged on a portfolio, not a single rank. In rough order of how fast they move:

1. **Technical eligibility** — crawl access, rendering, indexability, structured data, freshness, machine readability. Fixable in weeks, and usually the first real finding.
2. **Presence** — share of relevant prompts where the brand appears at all, across engines.
3. **Citation share** — which sources the engines pull from, and whether any of them are ours or ones we can influence.
4. **Representation quality** — is what the engines say about the brand accurate, complete, and positioned the way the company would want. Frequently the most alarming thing a first audit surfaces.
5. **Competitive share of voice** — presence relative to named competitors, by topic and engine.
6. **Referral and assisted demand** — AI-referred sessions, engaged visits, conversions, branded-search lift, self-reported attribution.
7. **Traditional search performance** — rankings, impressions, clicks, conversions. Still matters, still moves.
8. **Business outcomes** — qualified pipeline, CAC, revenue.

Items 1–5 show movement inside a pilot window. Items 6–8 mostly don't, and it's better to say so upfront than to be asked at week eight.

One methodological point worth stating, because it signals we know what we're doing: every AI-visibility measurement has to record its prompt set, sampling frequency, geography, personalisation state, and model version. Answers change when any of those change. A number without that context isn't a measurement.

### The value model, if someone wants a business case

> **Annual value = capacity saved + avoided external spend + incremental gross profit**
>
> — *capacity saved* = verified hours saved × loaded hourly cost
> — *avoided external spend* = tools, contractors or agency scope actually retired
> — *incremental gross profit* = attributable incremental revenue × gross margin
>
> Measured after subscription, implementation and internal operating cost.

The useful framing for a 20–100 person SaaS: the realistic in-house alternative is one mid-level hire. Indeed's US average for an SEO Manager is **$89,098 base** (319 postings, updated 31 July 2026) — call it **$115k–$130k fully loaded**, plus $5k–$15k a year in tooling. And one generalist still can't cover technical, content, AI-search and analytics, so in practice it's that hire plus contractor top-up, landing around **$140k–$160k a year**.

One person, one number, one dated citation. That version survives a CFO.

## Part 5 — Before you send it

### Don't say

- **"AEO" or "FDM" as jargon.** Say "AI search" and "a dedicated marketer." Part 2 uses AEO once and defines it — cut it if the reader isn't already in the vocabulary.
- **Anything about our internal stack.** The orchestration and inference layer is internal, not a selling point.
- **"A better Ahrefs" or "an agency."** We're neither, and claiming either invites the wrong comparison.
- **Any hedge on our own offer** — "we're still figuring out pricing," "we're early stage so..." Never.
- **Numbers on pricing, in writing.** See below.
- **A universal conversion multiplier.** See Part 4. Tempting, indefensible.
- **Our own gaps, unprompted.** If asked directly, be honest — backlink automation in particular is limited. Acknowledge briefly, move to what we do strongly.

### Two things to settle first

**Pricing.** Not fixed. Working anchors, untested: **$499–$999/mo** where a hands-on founder owns search, **$2,500–$5,000/mo** where a marketing leader with a team and budget owns it. Design-partner discount 30–50% off. Keep numbers out of anything written; if it comes up live, state a tier and connect it to what they're replacing. Context: the market has real price competition now — Gushwork publishes from ~$800/mo with no human, AthenaHQ starts at $295/mo, Profound went demo-led enterprise. Our founder tier sits in a crowded band.

**Naming customers.** We have live accounts — a Bangalore clinic and a SaaS company, both at $499/mo on Berlin plus a dedicated marketer, and an enterprise identity/security SaaS in compliance due diligence. Get permission before naming any of them in something that leaves our hands.

### The frame, in one line

The market runs **tools** on one side (Ahrefs, Semrush, Profound, Peec — leverage for someone who does the work themselves) and **agencies** on the other (done-for-you for teams with no search function). We're the middle: a platform the team operates on *plus* a dedicated marketer who executes on it with them. Co-equal — not a tool with support attached.

> "Berlin is a platform your team operates on plus a dedicated marketer who executes on it with you — the middle ground between buying a tool and hiring an agency. The platform handles strategy, execution and the tooling underneath; the marketer drives it alongside your team in a space moving too fast to keep up with solo."

### The questions you'll get

**"How is this different from hiring an agency?"**
> An agency gives you people who use tools to do the work — and most agencies are behind on the tech; they don't have agents. Most aren't even charging separately for AI-search work, which tells you how much of it is actually getting done. Berlin gives you a platform with AI agents doing the volume plus a dedicated marketer who runs it with you. Same output an agency promises — strategy, content, technical fixes, reporting — faster, fully transparent in a dashboard you operate, without the retainer lock-in.

**"Why wouldn't I just hire someone in-house?"**
> The realistic hire is a mid-level SEO manager — about $89k base on the US average, so $115k–$130k fully loaded, plus tooling, and three months to ramp. And one generalist can't cover technical, content, AI-search and analytics, so you end up topping up with contractors anyway. Berlin replaces that output on day one. When you do eventually hire, Berlin makes them several times more productive — it isn't wasted spend.

**"Isn't this just a wrapper on ChatGPT doing SEO?"**
> No. Berlin has its own orchestration, its own ranking-signal stack, its own crawl and keyword data, its own execution pipelines. LLMs are one component of many. The difference is that Berlin ships the work — writes, publishes, updates, monitors — not just generates suggestions.

**"How is this different from Profound?"**
> Profound is a good product aimed at a different customer. They're a $1B-valuation enterprise company selling demo-led into Fortune 500 marketing orgs that already have the people to act on what the platform surfaces. We're built for a team that doesn't have those people — the platform does the volume and a dedicated marketer executes it with you. Different buyer, different price, different amount of work you have to do yourself.

**"Isn't AI search still tiny? It's 1% of my traffic."**
> Today, yes — around 0.25% on a broad web average, more like 1.5% in software and financial services. Three reasons it still matters. It's growing far faster than anything else in the mix. The visitors convert better in most sectors we have data for. And AI Overviews shape decisions without producing a referral at all, so the trackable number understates the influence. The cost of starting now is low; the cost of starting once your competitors are the cited source is high.

**One note on how we differentiate.** Don't lead with "we execute and they only monitor." That was true a year ago and isn't any more — Profound, Conductor, AthenaHQ and Botify all ship execution now, and Profound's own site is organised as Monitor / Create / Operate. It's still fair to say Peec, Otterly and Ahrefs Brand Radar are read-only dashboards. But our real edge is **who we execute for**: the platforms that do the work are built for companies that already have staff to direct them. We're for the team that doesn't.
