v1.0 · internal

Everyone types something before they buy.

Thousands of those somethings land in front of us every month. This is the system we use to sort them — so that any two people looking at the same query reach the same conclusion about who typed it and what they need next.

This is the language layer. Anyone talking about buckets, axes, awareness stages or question types — in a report, a spec, a sales call or a database column — is using the vocabulary defined here, and nowhere else. What we then do with a classified query lives in the product spec, not on this page.

01 — What we deliberately left out

We only look at one window of the customer's life.

A customer's relationship with us runs from "doesn't know they have a problem" all the way to "renewing for the third year." The framework covers four of those stages and ignores the rest. That's a choice, not an oversight — here's the whole timeline, with the cuts shown.

There's a fifth Schwartz stage — Unaware — that we drop for a practical reason rather than a philosophical one: unaware people don't search for the problem, so there is nothing to classify. Everything to the right of "Most aware" is real, valuable work — it just belongs to support and lifecycle, not to this framework.

The filter runs first, every time. Before anyone assigns a single label, the query has to survive the scope check. If it fails, it doesn't get a partial label — it leaves the dataset.

One more scoping decision worth knowing: we classify query clusters, not individual keywords. A cluster is a group of queries that mean the same thing and would be answered by the same page. "seo agency cost", "how much do seo agencies charge" and "seo agency pricing" are one unit of work, not three. Individual keywords get classified only when clustering isn't available yet.

02 — The shape of the system

Depth, subject and form.

Every query that survives the filter gets three labels. Each one reads a single thing off the query and settles a single property of the answer that would win it.

Awareness stage
Sets the depth
How much do they already know?

Four stages, from "I have a symptom" to "I know your company and I'm ready." Decides how much the answer has to explain before it can say anything useful.

Question type
Sets the subject
What are they asking about?

Eight subjects an answer can cover. Diagnosing a problem, comparing options, justifying a price, surviving due diligence. Decides what the answer has to say.

Search intent
Sets the form
What kind of result would satisfy them?

Four actions. Reading, shopping, buying, or reaching a specific page. Decides the shape the answer takes — an article, a comparison, a form.

Depth, subject and form are three properties of the same answer, each settled by one of the three labels. That's the sentence to remember. Sections 03 and 04 work through them one at a time.

Keeping the three separate is the whole trick. If you find yourself deriving one label from another, something has gone wrong — and the next two sections exist to show why the derivation never actually holds.

There's also a fourth, lighter label — Brand presence — which just records whose name appears in the query: none, our_brand, competitor_brand or multiple_brands. It's useful context, but it isn't a core classification, it doesn't multiply the count, and it does not decide the other three. A category name is not a brand.

A note on names, used consistently from here on: Northstar stands in for whichever brand we're working for, and Acme, Corvus and Halden for its competitors. The product spec uses the same placeholders, so examples read the same way in both.

03 — Why there are three, not two

Question type is the one people try to delete.

It's the least self-evident of the three. Once you know how much someone knows and what kind of result they want, it isn't obvious what a third cut adds. Here is what it adds.

Four questions from someone shopping for a CRM. All four are solution-aware. All four are commercial. On a two-label grid they are the same query.

They need different pages.

A fit guide, a pricing page, a migration FAQ and a category comparison. No single asset serves all four, so a label that gives one answer where four are needed is a broken label.

They're likely answered from different places.

Comparisons largely from review sites and community threads. Pricing from the brand's own page or an aggregator. Risk questions from documentation and support forums. If that holds, “where is the answer coming from” has a different answer in each case — and only this label exposes it. Working expectation — to be tested per client, not assumed

You can win one and be invisible in three.

A two-label grid averages the four into a single mediocre figure and hides the split. Nobody learns that they own every pricing question and appear in no comparison at all.

That last point is the commercial argument. The blended cell is exactly what stops a client from discovering the one finding they would have paid for.
Awareness stage and search intent tell you how to talk to someone.
Question type tells you what to actually talk about.
04 — How the labels combine

The three labels don't move together.

The instinct is that these are the same measurement wearing three hats — that early-stage people read, late-stage people buy, and it's one clean diagonal from left to right. It isn't. That assumption is the single most expensive mistake you can make with this framework, so it's worth seeing it break.

“Hire an enterprise SEO agency.” The word hire makes this unmistakably transactional — this person is ready to spend money today. But they haven't named a single provider, which means they are only solution-aware. If you read intent as a proxy for awareness, you'd send them a signup page. What they actually need is help choosing. Readiness to act and depth of knowledge are two different meters.

Notice the one cell that's blacked out. "Northstar login" is a real, high-volume, unmistakably navigational query from someone who is maximally aware of us — and it's out of scope, because they're already a customer. The filter from section 01 reaches into the grid and removes it.

Past solution-aware, a named provider is usually what marks the shift, so awareness and brand presence tend to move together. They stay separate labels: a brand in the query is evidence of awareness rather than a reading of it.

Notice that the last two blocks show three values, not four. No most-aware row in one, no navigational row in the other. That's not an oversight. Not every question type reaches every awareness stage, and navigational queries are rare and mostly out of scope anyway. It's the same point as the caveat on 128: that number is what the labels can express, not what any company actually generates.

When you can't decide: the shift test.

Alter the query slightly and see which label moves. Alter what the searcher is assumed to know already → the awareness stage shifted. Alter what the answer has to be about → the question type shifted. Alter the kind of result that would satisfy them → the intent shifted. If nothing shifts, the label was never really in doubt.

05 — The full vocabulary

Every value we can assign, on one page.

Sixteen labels — four awareness stages, eight question types, four search intents. That's the complete vocabulary; nothing outside this list is a valid answer. Every row is visible at once so you can see the shape of each axis; open one for the definition, a real query, the work it implies, and the mistake people most often make with it.

Two lines are worth reading before you open anything. Awareness is separated from product-aware by named options, not by comparison — and it is never read off the tone of the wording. Intent is about the result wanted, not the subject — which is why the same pricing question appears at three different intents.

Brand presence is deliberately absent from this list. It isn't a judgement call — it's read straight off the query text — so it doesn't need a vocabulary anyone has to learn.

The “What we'd make” line on each row is a proposed extension, not part of framework v1.0 — it's the bridge from classification to a content brief, and it's the piece most worth arguing about.

Four stages × eight question types × four intents.

Position or bucket? A position is a coordinate in this space — one of the 128 combinations the labels can express. A bucket is the set of real query clusters that actually lands on one of them for a particular company and topic. There are always 128 positions; there are rarely 128 non-empty buckets. The product spec works in buckets because it deals in real client data; this page works in positions because it defines the coordinate system.

One caveat on the number. 128 is the count of positions the labels can express, not a claim that every one holds real demand. For any given company and topic a good many will be empty, plenty of others are rare or commercially dead, and part of the next phase is finding out which. The point of the picture is that the space is wide and structured, rather than a single line from "cold" to "hot."

09 — The rules, and what gets stored

Eight rules and one record.

Everything above is the reasoning. This is the part you keep open while you work.

0
Apply the scope filter before classifying anything. A query that fails it leaves the dataset rather than receiving a partial label.
1
Choose one primary awareness stage, question type and search intent.
2
Add a secondary intent or question type only when the query genuinely performs two jobs.
3
Do not derive any label from any other — and in particular, do not infer awareness from search intent, question type or brand presence.
4
Classify the dominant meaning of the cluster, not isolated keyword modifiers. “Cheap CRM” and “affordable CRM software” are the same question.
5
When meaning or intent is ambiguous, inspect what the engines actually return. The results usually settle it.
6
Prefer a consistent, explainable label over false precision.
7
Record unresolved cases so the taxonomy can improve.

The minimum record

Four required fields and one recommended. Anything a tool, a database or a report stores about a classified query starts here.

query_cluster: "best SEO agency for B2B SaaS" awareness_stage: solution_aware question_type: fit_and_constraints search_intent: commercial brand_presence: none # recommended, not required — auto-filled where reliable
10 — What's settled and what isn't

It's v1.0, and it's still meant to be argued with.

The eight question types are the least settled part. Eight is not a law of nature. It's our current best cut — built on what the industry already treats as standard, adapted from a review of example questions, and not yet validated against a real client's data. If client data keeps producing questions that fit none of the eight, we add a type and the position count changes. That's expected, and worth saying out loud rather than defending the number 128.

The source-of-answer claim in section 03 is a working expectation, not a finding. That comparisons get answered from review sites, pricing from brand pages and risk questions from documentation is the most important open question here — because it's what turns question type from a content-planning label into a discovery tool. It's testable against real client data, and worth testing early.

Two exclusions are decisions rather than conclusions. Post-purchase questions are out of scope; bringing them in would mean a ninth question type. Geography is collected separately, but “near me” style questions carry no location in the text itself, which is unsolved for multi-location brands. Both are tracked as open questions in the product spec.
